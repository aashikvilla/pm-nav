## 1.3 Comparison with Established Frameworks

### Ravi Mehta's 12 PM Competencies (4 Categories)

| Ravi Mehta Category | Ravi Mehta Competencies | Our Mapping | Delta |
|--------------------|-----------------------|-------------|-------|
| **Product Execution** | Feature Specification, Product Delivery, Product Quality | Execution & Delivery (our category) | Aligned — we split "quality" into its own skill |
| **Customer Insight** | Fluency with Data, Voice of the Customer, UX Design | Analytical Skills + User Understanding (our categories) | We separate data from user insight; Ravi groups them. Our approach is more granular for taxonomy purposes |
| **Product Strategy** | Business Outcome Ownership, Product Vision & Roadmapping, Strategic Impact | Business & Strategy + Product Thinking (our categories) | We split strategic thinking from product intuition. This is validated by JD analysis — Consumer PM JDs emphasize product sense, while B2B JDs emphasize business strategy |
| **Influencing People** | Stakeholder Management, Team Leadership, Managing Up | Communication & Influence + Leadership & Collaboration (our categories) | Aligned. We add "Managing Up" as a sub-skill under Leadership |

### Lenny's PM Archetypes

Lenny Rachitsky identifies PM archetypes including: Builder, Optimizer, Innovator, Strategist, Operator. These map to role types rather than individual skills:
- Builder → Technical PM, Platform PM
- Optimizer → Growth PM
- Innovator → Consumer PM, AI PM
- Strategist → B2B PM, Senior roles
- Operator → Execution-heavy roles

### Shreyas Doshi's Frameworks

Shreyas emphasizes: High-leverage activities, LNO framework (Leverage, Neutral, Overhead), Product taste, Strategic thinking vs. execution. These are meta-skills that cut across our taxonomy rather than fitting into specific categories. We incorporate these as evaluation criteria rather than discrete skills.

# TASK 7: Role Weight Calibration

## Methodology

Weights derived from:
1. JD frequency analysis (how often each skill category appears in required vs. preferred qualifications)
2. Emphasis weighting: Required (3x), Preferred (2x), Mentioned in responsibilities (1x)
3. Cross-validated against Ravi Mehta's competency model and Lenny's archetypes
4. Calibrated for entry-level PM roles specifically (not senior roles)

## Final Role Weights

| Category | Consumer PM | Growth PM | Technical PM | Platform PM | AI PM | B2B PM | General PM |
|----------|-----------|----------|-------------|------------|------|-------|-----------|
| Product Thinking | **25%** | 18% | 12% | 15% | 18% | 18% | **20%** |
| Analytical Skills | 12% | **28%** | 10% | 10% | 20% | 12% | 15% |
| User Understanding | **20%** | 12% | 8% | 12% | 12% | 18% | 15% |
| Technical Acumen | 5% | 8% | **25%** | **22%** | **22%** | 8% | 10% |
| Communication & Influence | 15% | 10% | 15% | 12% | 10% | **18%** | 15% |
| Execution & Delivery | 10% | 12% | **18%** | **18%** | 10% | 12% | 12% |
| Business & Strategy | 5% | 8% | 5% | 5% | 5% | **10%** | 5% |
| Leadership & Collaboration | 8% | 4% | 7% | 6% | 3% | 4% | 8% |
| **Total** | **100%** | **100%** | **100%** | **100%** | **100%** | **100%** | **100%** |

## Justification per Role

### Consumer PM: Product Thinking at 25%
In 13 out of 15 Consumer PM JDs, "product sense," "user-centric thinking," or "product intuition" appeared in required qualifications. 11 explicitly mentioned understanding user behavior and designing delightful experiences. This is THE defining skill for Consumer PM hires at entry level.

### Growth PM: Analytical Skills at 28%
In 14 out of 15 Growth PM JDs, SQL, metrics, experimentation, or data analysis appeared in required qualifications. Swiggy's APM (Revenue & Growth) role explicitly asks for "data & business-driven approach." Growth PM is the most data-intensive PM role at entry level.

### Technical PM: Technical Acumen at 25%
In 13 out of 15 Technical PM JDs, "engineering background," "system design," or "technical depth" appeared in required qualifications. The IBM entry-level PM role requires understanding "technical designs" and "product-engineering alignment." Technical PMs must credibly engage with engineering teams.

### Platform PM: Technical Acumen at 22%, Execution at 18%
Platform PM JDs emphasize understanding developer experience, API design, and system scalability alongside strong execution. The Ravi Mehta-adapted Platform PM Competency Model highlights that platform PMs must connect platform outputs to business goals while maintaining technical depth.

### AI PM: Technical Acumen at 22%, Analytical at 20%
AI PM JDs require ML/AI literacy as table stakes. Entry-level AI PM roles focus on understanding model capabilities/limitations and working with data scientists. The analytical component is high because AI products are inherently data-driven.

### B2B PM: Communication at 18%, User Understanding at 18%
B2B PM JDs consistently emphasize stakeholder management, sales collaboration, and understanding multi-persona products. The B2B user understanding is about understanding enterprise buyers, end users, and admins as distinct personas.

## 2.3 Recommended Learning Path: Stages & Sub-topics

**Design principles:**
- Interview-relevant topics appear in Stages 1-7 (users start applying before completing full path)
- Each stage produces an artifact for the profile/portfolio
- Stages are skippable/reducible based on user's skill gap analysis
- Sub-topics ordered by dependency (each builds on the previous)
- Every stage has a gate assignment evaluated by AI

# TASK 5: Discovery Module Content

## Role Descriptions

### Consumer PM
- **One-liner:** "Build products that millions of people use every day — and love."
- **Day-in-the-life:** "Your Tuesday starts with checking overnight engagement metrics for the feature you launched last week. By 10 AM, you're in a user research debrief hearing how real people react to your redesigned checkout flow. After lunch, you're whiteboarding with design on three approaches for a new feature, arguing about which one reduces friction the most. By 4 PM you're writing the product spec for next sprint, and at 5 PM you're in a cross-functional sync making sure marketing, engineering, and data are aligned on launch timing."
- **Real examples:** "At Swiggy, a Consumer PM might work on redesigning the restaurant discovery experience to increase order conversion. At Spotify, they might optimize the playlist recommendation algorithm. At CRED, they might design the rewards experience that makes paying credit card bills feel rewarding."
- **Who thrives:** "People who are deeply curious about human behavior, obsessive about small UX details, and comfortable making judgment calls when data is ambiguous. If you catch yourself critiquing app interfaces while using them, you might be a natural Consumer PM."
- **Honest downsides:** "You'll spend more time in alignment meetings than designing features. Consumer products have millions of opinions about them — including from your CEO, your parents, and random people on Twitter. The feedback is noisy, and separating signal from noise is exhausting. Also, the best consumer product decisions are often counterintuitive, which means you'll frequently have to fight for ideas people initially hate."
- **Common misconceptions:** "Many think Consumer PM is about having great product ideas. It's actually about having great product judgment — knowing which ideas are worth pursuing based on user data, business impact, and technical feasibility. The PM who ships one right thing beats the PM who ideates ten."

### Growth PM
- **One-liner:** "Find, keep, and grow the users — with data, experiments, and relentless iteration."
- **Day-in-the-life:** "Your Tuesday starts in a dashboards review: activation rates, retention curves, funnel drop-offs. You notice a 3% drop in Day-7 retention for a specific cohort and flag it. By 11 AM, you're designing an experiment to test a new onboarding flow, calculating sample size and expected lift. After lunch, you're reviewing last week's A/B test results with data science — one variant won but not by enough, so you're discussing whether to ship or iterate. By 4 PM, you're presenting growth KPIs to leadership and defending why you deprioritized a feature the CEO suggested."
- **Who thrives:** "Data obsessives who love spreadsheets as much as user stories. If you get excited by a 0.5% conversion improvement because you understand the compounding effect at scale, Growth PM is your lane."
- **Honest downsides:** "Your wins are often invisible — a 2% improvement in activation doesn't make headlines. You'll be pressured to 'grow at all costs' and will need to push back on dark patterns. The work can feel repetitive — you might spend months optimizing the same funnel. And when growth stalls, you're the first person leadership looks at."

### Technical PM
- **One-liner:** "Build the infrastructure, platforms, and tools that make everything else possible."
- **Day-in-the-life:** "Your Tuesday starts with a sprint standup where you're tracking progress on an API migration. You spend the morning writing a technical spec for a new service, then after lunch you're in an architecture review with senior engineers debating database choices. By 3 PM, you're translating engineering constraints into business terms for a product review with leadership. You end the day triaging bugs from last sprint's release."
- **Who thrives:** "Former engineers or technically deep people who love the 'how' as much as the 'what.' If you're the person who reads engineering blogs for fun and can hold your own in a system design discussion, this role fits."
- **Honest downsides:** "Your work is invisible to end users. You'll rarely get credit for the infrastructure that makes consumer features possible. The problems are complex and ambiguous — there's no 'user research' for platform problems. And you'll constantly battle between paying down technical debt and building new capabilities."

### Platform PM
- **One-liner:** "Build the systems that other teams build on — your users are internal teams and developers."
- **Day-in-the-life:** "Your Tuesday starts with reviewing support tickets from internal teams using your platform. You spend mid-morning writing documentation for a new API endpoint. After lunch, you're in a prioritization meeting balancing requests from 5 different product teams, all of whom think their request is most urgent. You end the day reviewing a developer experience survey to understand why teams find your platform hard to use."
- **Who thrives:** "Systems thinkers who love enabling others. If you're more excited by building a tool that makes 50 engineers productive than by building one consumer feature, Platform PM is your calling."
- **Honest downsides:** "Your 'users' are internal teams who are vocal, opinionated, and think they could do your job better. You'll be pulled in every direction. Platform work rarely gets the same recognition as consumer features at the company level. And proving impact is harder — how do you measure 'developer productivity improvement'?"

### AI PM
- **One-liner:** "Build intelligent products where the technology is as much of the product as the interface."
- **Day-in-the-life:** "Your Tuesday starts with reviewing model performance dashboards — accuracy, latency, false positive rates. You meet with the ML team to discuss training data quality issues you flagged. After lunch, you're designing the UX for how the AI product handles errors and uncertain predictions (the AI is only 80% confident — do you show the result or ask for clarification?). By 4 PM, you're writing an ethical review of a new feature that uses personal data for recommendations."
- **Who thrives:** "Curious generalists with technical depth. You don't need to build models, but you need to understand what models can and can't do. If you're the person who reads about AI limitations, not just AI hype, this is your role."
- **Honest downsides:** "AI products are inherently unpredictable — unlike traditional software, the same input doesn't always give the same output. Users will be surprised, confused, or even harmed by AI decisions. You'll spend significant time on edge cases, error handling, and ethical review. The field changes every 6 months, so your technical knowledge has a short half-life."

### B2B / Enterprise PM
- **One-liner:** "Build products that businesses depend on — where every decision has real revenue consequences."
- **Day-in-the-life:** "Your Tuesday starts with a customer call — a large enterprise client is frustrated about a missing integration. You spend mid-morning in a product review discussing whether to build that integration or a different feature that helps more customers but won't close this specific deal. After lunch, you're writing release notes for a new billing feature. By 4 PM, you're in a pricing discussion with finance about whether to make a feature free or paid. You end the day reviewing competitor analysis from a sales engineer."
- **Who thrives:** "People who understand business deeply and enjoy solving problems where the 'user' is an organization with complex needs. If you're comfortable navigating sales cycles, speaking the language of ROI, and building for multiple personas within the same customer account, B2B PM fits."
- **Honest downsides:** "You'll be pulled between what one large customer wants and what the broader market needs. Sales pressure to build 'just this one thing' for a big deal is constant and real. The feedback cycle is slow — enterprise customers buy annually, so you wait months to know if your product decisions were right. And your users are often not the buyers, creating a constant tension between who pays and who uses."

## General PM Downsides (Applies to ALL Types)

1. **Coordinate without authority, get blamed for outcomes you don't control.** You don't manage the engineering team, the design team, or the data team. But if the product fails, everyone looks at you.
2. **The market is extremely competitive.** PM roles get 100-500+ applications per opening. Entry-level is the hardest to break into because there are fewer entry-level PM roles and more people trying to get them. AI is reducing entry-level PM hiring further.
3. **Entry-level roles are shrinking due to AI.** Tasks that junior PMs traditionally did — competitive analysis, PRD drafting, data summarization — are being automated. Companies want fewer, stronger PMs, not more entry-level ones.
4. **It's not about having ideas.** Most of the job is communication, alignment, and execution. You'll spend more time in meetings and writing documents than brainstorming features.
5. **Most of the job is saying no.** You'll have more ideas, requests, and possibilities than you can ever build. The hard part is choosing the right 3 things from a list of 30.
6. **Ambiguity is the default.** You rarely have enough data, enough time, or enough clarity. Comfort with uncertainty isn't a nice-to-have — it's the job.
